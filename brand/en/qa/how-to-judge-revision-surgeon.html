How do you judge whether a revision rhinoplasty surgeon is reliable?

Wang Ting · Associate Chief Physician · updated 2026-11-23

Check three things: the revision share, imaging and the plan, and how complications are handled. A surgeon who does 300 nasal operations a year with only 20 revisions is doing revision on the side. Ask at least five questions at consultation; anyone who cannot answer them is out.

Revision rhinoplasty and primary rhinoplasty are two different operations. Primary adds to normal structure; revision rebuilds structure that has been damaged. The same operation can differ three times over in difficulty. So the standard for judging a surgeon has to be different too.

1. Look at the revision share, not the total

A surgeon who does 300 nasal operations a year with 20 revisions is doing revision on the side. One who does 100 a year with 60 revisions is doing revision as the main work.

Ask for the specific numbers. Do not accept “I do a lot of them” as an answer.

2. Ask for imaging and a plan, not just case photos

Revision starts with knowing what the last operation did: implant type, where cartilage was taken, how much septum is left.

A responsible surgeon asks for the previous operative record, orders a nasal CT, or at least standardised photos from several angles, before talking about a plan.

Anyone who shows a few retouched before-and-after photos and pushes for a deposit can be left.

3. Ask what happens if it does not work

This question filters best. Revision has a failure rate. A mature surgeon states the complication rate, the window for a second adjustment and how the cost is calculated.

An immature one says “it will not fail”.

4. Check who is talking to you

You must meet the operating surgeon in person at consultation.

If a consultant presents the plan the whole time and the doctor appears only on the day of surgery, the plan was not set by the doctor.

5. Is the price tiered and written down

Revision is priced by difficulty. Removing an implant and rebuilding the framework are not the same price.

A surgeon willing to give a range and the basis for the tiers before consultation is usually also willing to take responsibility afterwards.

Five questions to ask at consultation

1. How many revision rhinoplasties did you do last year, and what share of your nasal operations was that?

2. What material was used in my last operation, and how do you plan to deal with it?

3. How much septum do I have left, and is it enough? If not, what is the alternative?

4. If this result is not good, what is the time window and the cost for a second adjustment?

5. Will you do the operation yourself, or another doctor on your team?

Key facts

Check yourself: Revision share against total nasal operations; previous operative record plus CT or standardised photos; a clear answer on complications and cost; the operating surgeon at consultation; a tiered price and its basis.

Red flag: “I do a lot of them” with no number; polished photos instead of imaging; “it will not fail”; a consultant presenting the plan alone.
